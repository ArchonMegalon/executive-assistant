Rain made the clinic sign stutter.

Kestrel watched the last red letter die, come back wrong, then die again. The sign had once said WALK-INS WELCOME. Someone had pried away half the plastic faceplate, so now it blinked W LK  S W L O E above a door reinforced with three kinds of metal and one visible lie: a clean corporate keypad bolted to a wall that had never known a building inspector.

She kept one hand inside her jacket and the other pressed flat against the bandage below her collarbone. The cut under it was new enough to burn and old enough to itch. Under the gauze, beneath the bruising and the glue, the second-hand reflex booster waited like a borrowed animal.

Inside, Vela said, "If you're going to bleed on my threshold, do it all the way in."

Kestrel did not move.

The rain behind her was colder than the clinic air, but the street had rules she understood. Rain hid faces. Rain softened footfalls. Rain made even expensive drones blink twice before they trusted their own cameras. The clinic had different rules. The clinic had invoices. The clinic had the smell of disinfectant fighting mold and losing by inches. The clinic had Vela, who had cut Kestrel open three hours ago and put a machine near her spine because the alternative was a black bag and an organ auction.

"Cale's men came by," Kestrel said.

Vela's shadow shifted across the frosted glass. "Cale's men always come by. That is what makes them Cale's men."

"They asked whether I could run yet."

"Can you?"

Kestrel flexed her fingers. Her hand answered too fast. The motion snapped sharp, almost elegant, and pain flashed white behind her teeth. Somewhere in the cheap wiring above the door, a monitor chirped once. Her shoulders locked before she could stop them.

Vela opened the clinic door.

She was smaller than her voice, all brown hands and shaved scalp and chrome-framed lenses that made her eyes look like expensive evidence. The pistol in her left hand was not pointed at Kestrel. It was pointed through Kestrel, toward the alley mouth, where a black sedan idled without lights.

"You were told not to come back tonight," Vela said.

"You told me not to sprint, not to get shot, and not to make moral decisions while medicated."

"That covered tonight."

Kestrel looked past her. The surgical chair still sat under the lamp. A tray of clamps gleamed beside it. The reflex booster crate lay open on the floor, padded foam hollowed into the shape of the thing now living under her skin. On the far wall, a cracked AR panel looped the same three warnings in clinic orange: NERVE SYNC UNSTABLE, PAIN WATCH ACTIVE, TORQUE LIMIT LOW.

There should have been a fourth warning. DEBT NOW BREATHING.

"Mako has the ledger," Kestrel said.

Vela's pistol dipped one centimeter. "Mako is a courier. Couriers carry messages. They do not steal debt ledgers from men who buy people's futures wholesale."

"This one did."

"Then this one is already dead."

Kestrel heard the sedan door open behind her. Not the door itself; the weather swallowed that. She heard the change in the rain, the way drops struck a coat instead of pavement. The booster under her bandage wanted to turn her around before her thoughts arrived. It was eager. It was stupid. It did not understand that moving first was not the same as surviving.

Vela saw the calculation in her face. "No."

"He came for me after the squat burned."

"Mako came for everyone."

"Not everyone got out."

Vela stepped closer, just enough for the clinic light to touch the scar at Kestrel's collarbone. "Listen carefully. That hardware lets you react. It does not make you bulletproof, clever, paid, forgiven, or ready."

"I know."

"You don't. That's why you're standing in my doorway with a fresh incision and a hero complex."

The alley spoke in a man's voice. "Kestrel."

Mr. Cale never raised his voice. He had other people for that. Tonight he stood under a clear umbrella held by a boy too young to hate him properly. Cale wore a black coat that repelled the rain so completely the droplets looked embarrassed to touch it. Two men flanked him. Both had their hands empty. That was worse than guns.

"Clinic hours are over," Vela called.

Cale smiled at her as if she had made a useful joke. "Doctor, I paid for part of that installation."

"You paid for the right to wait outside."

"I paid for collateral."

Kestrel felt the booster shiver in her nerves. She imagined Mako running three blocks west with a stolen ledger under his jacket, bleeding from a place he would not mention until he fell down. She imagined the first crew she had lost: Jinx laughing over comms, Rabbit swearing at a locked service door, Sol with one hand clamped over a wound and the other waving her away. Go, Sol had said. That was the first rule the shadows had taught her. Go when staying costs more than you can pay.

It was a bad rule.

Kestrel stepped inside the clinic.

Vela's mouth tightened. "Good."

Then Kestrel took the surgical tray in both hands and hurled it through the hallway window.

The crash made the alley turn its head. For one second the clinic became rain, glass, orange warning light, and Vela shouting a word Kestrel had never heard but understood perfectly. Kestrel moved through the broken window before the second second arrived.

The booster fired wrong. Her legs became lightning attached to meat. Pain tore a hot seam from her collarbone to the base of her skull, but the alley stretched open around her. Cale's men reached for weapons that had been invisible a heartbeat earlier. Vela fired once from the doorway, not at Cale and not at the men, but at the sedan's front tire. Rubber burst. The boy with the umbrella dropped it and ran.

Kestrel hit the side wall, bounced, caught a rusted ladder, and climbed because climbing was not sprinting if you lied to your own body with enough conviction. Her left hand missed the third rung. Her right hand caught the fourth. The booster corrected. Her shoulder screamed. She went up.

Below, Cale said, "The debt follows you."

Kestrel looked down from the fire escape. Rain striped her face. Clinic light burned behind Vela, turning the streetdoc into a dark cutout with a smoking pistol and no patience left.

"Then it better learn to run," Kestrel said.

She crossed the roofline toward Mako, toward the stolen ledger, toward a courier who might already be dead and a rule she had not known she believed until it cost her blood.

Nobody gets sold.

Nobody gets left in the rain.
